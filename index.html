Australian International Education · 2019 to 2025

Many origins, one destination

International students in Australia, 2019 to 2025

International education is one of Australia's biggest export industries and a defining part of life in cities like Sydney and Melbourne. But “international students” is not one story. It is many. This page follows them: where they come from, where they study, and how the picture changed after the pandemic.

01

The big picture

From crash to record.

Over the past six years, Australia's international education sector has weathered one of the most disruptive periods in its modern history. From a pre-pandemic peak of around 950,000 enrolments, numbers fell sharply to a low of 716,000 in 2021 as the country's borders stayed closed to international students for almost two years.

By 2024, with borders fully reopened, the sector had not only recovered but reached a new record of 1.09 million students. A modest dip then followed in 2025 after the federal government proposed enrolment caps for the first time, slowing the arrival of new students.

02

Six countries, half the students

So where do these students actually come from? The map below shows the world recoloured by enrolment count.

Australia's international students come overwhelmingly from one part of the world. A clear arc runs from China through India, the subcontinent, and Southeast Asia, joined by a smaller cluster from Colombia and Brazil. Most of Africa, Europe, and North America barely register. The recruiting ground for Australian international education is far narrower than a world map would suggest.

A two-horse race, then a long tail

Two countries are in a league of their own: China sent 227,000 students in 2025, India sent 182,000. The more striking finding is third place. Nepal sent nearly 90,000 students, more than Vietnam, Colombia, and Indonesia combined. Below the top three the numbers drop off sharply, with most countries on the list sending fewer than 30,000 students each. But these ranks aren't fixed: the mix has been shifting for years.

A widening mix

The mix has been visibly shifting for years. China's share of the total is shrinking in relative terms, even as overall numbers reach new records. India, the Philippines, and a widening “Other” tail are expanding to fill the space. Australian international education is becoming markedly more diverse than it was before the pandemic.

03

Two states host two-thirds

The map shows international students per 100,000 residents. New South Wales and Victoria sit at the top, drawing students at a rate well above every other state. The bigger surprise is the Australian Capital Territory: with a population a fraction of either, Canberra hosts students at nearly the same rate. Tasmania, by contrast, trails the country. Knowing where students go is only half the picture. What they study tells a different story.

Not all "students" are at university

Higher Education is the largest sector at 52%, but vocational education comes in at a serious 34%. A third of all international students in Australia are doing trades, hospitality, aged care, or similar courses, not university degrees. The remaining 14% study English-language courses (ELICOS), attend schools, or take non-award short courses. This national average hides a lot of variation.

The mix is not the same everywhere

New South Wales and Victoria look like "university states": over half of their international students are in Higher Education. Western Australia, Tasmania, and South Australia tell a different story, with a much larger share of their students in vocational courses, in fields like trades, hospitality, and aged care that respond to local industry needs. There is no single international-student profile by state.

04

Not one student body — four

"International students" doesn't describe one phenomenon. It describes at least four. Cross-tabulating each country against the education sector its students enrol in reveals four distinct patterns. Chinese students concentrate in Higher Education. Filipino students go overwhelmingly into vocational training. Colombian and Brazilian students follow a language-then-skills pathway, starting in ELICOS and moving into VET. Indian and Nepalese students split between degrees and VET. Each row of the heatmap shows a different relationship with Australian education.

Specific pipelines

Sector tells you the level of study. Field tells you the subject. Management & Commerce is the largest field for almost every country, but the deeper patterns are in the second and third bars of each panel. Information Technology is heavily India-driven. Health draws strongly on Filipino and Nepalese students. Engineering is concentrated in students from China, India, and Vietnam. Several of these pipelines mirror Australia's stated labour shortages.

Three pathways, roughly equal

Group the qualifications by the pathway they belong to and a surprising pattern emerges. Postgraduate study accounts for 31% of all enrolments, led by 268,000 Masters students. Vocational training is close behind at 28%, anchored by Diplomas and Certificate III/IV courses. Undergraduate study sits at 27%. The three groups are roughly equal in size, which is not what most Australians picture when they think of international students. The fourth tier — English-language courses and school-level study — accounts for the remaining 14%, often as a stepping stone into one of the three larger pathways.

05

The post-pandemic reshuffle

Comparing 2019 with 2025 shows who recovered and who did not. Most top-ten countries grew. The fastest growth came from Bangladesh, which more than quadrupled, and the Philippines, which more than doubled. The largest absolute gain came from India, which added 39,000 students. Two countries moved the other way: China is still below its pre-COVID peak, and Brazil is the only major source in sustained decline.

By indexing each state to its own 2019 level, we can see the shape of recovery without large states drowning out small ones. Most states overshot their pre-COVID baseline by 2024. Western Australia and the Northern Territory rebounded most dramatically, driven by strong VET demand. New South Wales recovered most modestly. Tasmania is the outlier, with international student numbers still falling.

06

Final Summary

Australia’s international student story is one of recovery, diversification, and concentration.

Over the past six years, the sector has weathered a global crisis and emerged larger than ever, with 1.06 million students enrolled in 2025. But the shape of that cohort has changed. China remains the largest single source but has not returned to its pre-COVID peak, while Bangladesh, the Philippines, and a long tail of smaller-source countries have grown sharply. Australian international education is more diverse than ever.

The destinations have stayed concentrated: New South Wales and Victoria still host two-thirds of all enrolments, with the Australian Capital Territory punching well above its weight per capita. But where students go is only part of the story — what they study reveals at least four distinct migration patterns, from Chinese postgraduates in business and engineering, to Filipino students in vocational health and aged care, to Latin American students moving through English-language pathways into trades.

The image of “international students” as a single group of university undergraduates is incomplete. The real cohort is a mix of three roughly equal pathways — postgraduate, undergraduate, and vocational — supported by a fourth tier of English-language and school-level study. Each pathway connects to a different part of the Australian economy, and each has its own geography, sector, and country profile.

What started as a single headline number — one million students — turns out, on closer inspection, to be many quieter stories.
